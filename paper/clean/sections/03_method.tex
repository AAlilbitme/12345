\section{Symbolic Model and Notation}\label{sec:symbolic}

We model the protocol using \tamarin~\cite{meier2013}, a symbolic tool for security-protocol
analysis. It tracks a \emph{system}, a multiset of facts called the state~$S$, which
evolves following user-defined transition \emph{rules}
$[\,L\,]\;\rule[0.55ex]{1.2em}{0.4pt}\,[\,A\,]\,\rightarrow\;[\,R\,]$: when the premises~$L$
unify with facts in~$S$, they are rewritten as the conclusions~$R$, and the action facts~$A$ are
appended to the trace. Linear facts are consumed upon rule application; persistent facts
(prefix \texttt{!}) can be reused arbitrarily often. The built-in facts \code{In},
\code{Out} and \code{Fr} capture receiving from and sending to a public (i.e.,
attacker-controlled) channel, and generating fresh, unique values. We use the hashing
($h$, for payment hashes $y = h(x)$ and the commitment $h(s)$ to a revocation secret~$s$),
signing (\code{sign}, \code{verify} and \code{pk}, for the channel handshake, the commitments and
the invoices) and natural-numbers built-ins (amounts, prefixed with \texttt{\%}, with addition,
for the per-hop fee constraint of Sec.~\ref{sec:core-amounts}). Security goals are first-order
formulas over traces of action facts (with time points \texttt{\#i}): \code{all-traces} lemmas
are security queries over all traces, \code{exists-trace} lemmas reachability queries. A \emph{restriction} is a formula of the
same kind that \tamarin{} imposes on the model: only traces satisfying every restriction are
considered.

\section{Formal Modelling Methodology}\label{sec:method}

We model the protocol as a single \tamarin{} theory, \code{lightning.spthy} (37 rules), with one
adversary. It contains both the payment-channel protocol (Sec.~\ref{sec:channel}) and
multi-hop routing (Sec.~\ref{sec:routing}); one generic rule, \code{Forward_HTLC}, handles every
hop, so routes of any length are covered. The routing layer does not abstract the
channel layer away, but the coupling is deliberately thin: commitment transactions carry no HTLC
outputs, so the channel state is independent of the payments, and the layers share only the
long-term keys and three explicit interfaces.

\paragraph{Topology and Slot} \code{Lock_Funds_And_Open} mints the persistent facts
\code{!ChannelConnect}, one per direction, which \code{Sender_Offer_HTLC}, \code{Forward_HTLC}
and \code{Open_Timeout} require, so HTLCs are added only on opened channels
(\code{HTLC_On_Opened_Channel}). It also mints one linear fact \code{Mutex(ptr)}, which adding an
HTLC consumes and redeeming or refunding that HTLC returns: a channel holds at most one
in-flight HTLC at a time and can be reused afterwards.

\paragraph{Resolution} Every rule that redeems or refunds an HTLC records the action fact
\code{Old}, and the restriction \code{OneResolutionPerHTLC} (Appendix~\ref{lst:structural}) lets
each HTLC be resolved at most once. This interface assumption holds in the protocol because an
HTLC output of the current commitment can be spent only once on chain, and spending it from an
older state requires publishing a revoked commitment, which the counterparty can punish
(\code{Publish_Revoked_A/B}, \code{Punish_A/B}). The argument is not mechanised, and we claim no
composition theorem; a mechanised discharge (by composition, or by refinement as
in~\cite{grundmann2026}) is the natural next step. All other abstractions are also explicit,
named restrictions (Sec.~\ref{sec:restrictions}).

\subsection{Threat Model}\label{sec:threat}

Our adversary is the standard Dolev-Yao adversary~\cite{dolevyao1983} of \tamarin{}: it
controls the whole network and can read, modify and inject messages of arbitrary
complexity within the term algebra. Public keys, channel proposals and acknowledgements, and
invoices are sent over the network. It also chooses the values honest rules read from it: the
balances of a channel update (\code{Update_State}) and each hop's incoming amount, fee and
outgoing amount (\code{Forward_HTLC}), subject only to the incoming amount being the outgoing
amount plus the fee. HTLC offers and fulfilments between adjacent hops are linear facts
(\code{Route}, \code{Fulfilled}), i.e., authenticated, non-replayable messages between channel
partners.

\paragraph{Key Compromise} Rule \code{Compromise_Ltk} reveals the long-term key of any party at
any time (\code{LtkCompromised}); the adversary can then sign as that party, e.g., forge its
invoices, so that a payment without an invoice of the receiver can settle
(\code{Forged_Invoice_Requires_Key_Compromise}). The preimage~$x$ is never sent on the network,
and the adversary never learns it (\code{Preimage_Never_Known}); instead, two compromised parties
with HTLCs on the same payment hash can relay~$x$ around the parties between them with the rule
\code{AttackerSkip} (Sec.~\ref{sec:core-skip}): the wormhole attack~\cite{malavolta2019}. Apart
from their keys, \code{AttackerSkip} and the exemptions of Sec.~\ref{sec:restrictions},
compromised parties follow the protocol rules.

\paragraph{Time and Liveness} Time is abstract: the timeout of channel \code{ptr} is the event
\code{TimedOut(ptr)} of rule \code{Open_Timeout}, which can happen at any point after the channel
opens, at most once per channel (\code{OneTimeoutPerPtr}), so the adversary chooses when
timeouts happen relative to honest actions. Controlling the network, it could also keep a party
from noticing fraud or sending the defending transaction, which trivially breaks
punishment (Sec.~\ref{par:punishment}) and claiming (Remark~\ref{rmk:claiming})~\cite{mccorry2019}.

\subsection{Liveness Restrictions}\label{sec:restrictions}

We therefore impose liveness on honest forwarders by three restrictions on the routing layer,
which limit the adversary's control over the order of events and rule out pathological honest
behaviour (the flags \code{NO_RBT}, \code{NO_OTF}, \code{NO_IMC} remove them for the necessity
runs):
\begin{spthy}[linerange={1-3,9-14,20-26}]
restriction RedeemBeforeTimeout:
    "All ptr y #i #j.
        Redeemed(ptr, y) @ #i & TimedOut(ptr) @ #j ==> #i < #j"
#endif

#ifdef NO_OTF
// restriction removed for the necessity check
#else
restriction OutgoingTimesOutFirst:
    "All P Prev Next ptrIn ptrOut y #f #k.
        HTLCForwarded(P, Prev, Next, ptrIn, ptrOut, y) @ #f
      & TimedOut(ptrIn) @ #k
      & not (Ex #c. LtkCompromised(P) @ #c & #c < #k)
     ==> Ex #m. TimedOut(ptrOut) @ #m & #m < #k"
#endif

#ifdef NO_IMC
// restriction removed for the necessity check
#else
restriction IntermediaryMustClaim:
    "All P Prev Next ptrIn ptrOut y #f #i #k.
        HTLCForwarded(P, Prev, Next, ptrIn, ptrOut, y) @ #f
      & Redeemed(ptrOut, y) @ #i
      & TimedOut(ptrIn)  @ #k
      & not (Ex #c. LtkCompromised(P) @ #c & #c < #k)
     ==> Ex #l. Redeemed(ptrIn, y) @ #l"
\end{spthy}
\noindent\textbf{T1} (\code{RedeemBeforeTimeout}) \emph{orders every redemption before the
timeout of its channel:} a well-formedness condition, not a liveness assumption.
\textbf{T2} (\code{OutgoingTimesOutFirst}) is the decreasing-deadline (CLTV-delta) invariant
of Remark~\ref{rmk:deadlines}: \emph{if the incoming HTLC of a hop times out and the
forwarder was not compromised before, its outgoing HTLC timed out earlier.} \textbf{T3}
(\code{IntermediaryMustClaim}) is the claiming liveness of Remarks~\ref{rmk:claiming}
and~\ref{rmk:deadlines}, on which intermediary safety rests: \emph{if a forwarder's outgoing
HTLC is redeemed and its incoming HTLC times out, and the forwarder was not compromised before
that timeout, the incoming one is redeemed too.} It only constrains traces with an incoming
timeout, the only ones that admit an upstream refund (\code{Refund_Requires_Timeout});
by T1, the claim then precedes the timeout.

\tamarin{} derives the order of a hop's two timeouts from T2 and \code{OneTimeoutPerPtr}
(\code{ForwardTimeGap}, Appendix~\ref{app:safety}): \emph{if both channels of a hop time out and
its forwarder is not compromised before the incoming timeout, the outgoing one times out
strictly first.} As restrictions, T1 to T3 remove every trace that violates them, including traces in which an uncompromised forwarder could not have acted in
time; our results say nothing about such traces (Sec.~\ref{par:liveness-scope}). The liveness of
punishment (Sec.~\ref{par:punishment}) is not encoded: no restriction mentions cheating or
punishment. Only T3 is necessary for intermediary safety (Sec.~\ref{sec:timing}). The bypass
theorem for routes of any length and a single offer per payment hash (Sec.~\ref{sec:bypass-any})
is proved without T1 to T3, all other results of the main theory under all nine restrictions
(six structural; Appendix~\ref{lst:structural}).

\subsection{Structural Limitations}\label{sec:structure}

Verifying the Lightning Network as one monolithic block hits the scalability limits of
verification tools~\cite{grundmann2026}, \tamarin{} included: with signatures, an equational
theory, natural-number induction and an unbounded clock, the search space explodes and
\tamarin{} exhausts memory; without the unbounded clock, the others are tractable together. The
main theory therefore represents timeouts as untimed events ordered by the restrictions above.
Two of the five small, self-contained models of \code{lightning_aux.spthy} (hashing and natural
numbers only, not composed with the main theory; Table~\ref{tab:results}) analyse the block
clock and the CLTV arithmetic; the others are the PTLC countermeasure, its HTLC baseline and the
early-timeout race.

Five deliberate non-goals buy generality or tractability. \emph{Onion privacy:} any channel
partner may be the next hop and nothing is encrypted in layers, so the results cover every route but
claim no privacy. \emph{On-chain fees:} publishing a transaction is free,
separating protocol logic from fee economics. \emph{Fee policy:} the adversary chooses
every routing fee, so the results hold for any policy. \emph{Wallet capacity:} HTLC amounts are
not checked against balances, so the results hold for all amounts. \emph{Per-HTLC CSV
stage:} HTLCs are resolved in one step, with no second-stage transaction.
