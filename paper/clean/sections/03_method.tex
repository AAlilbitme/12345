\section{Symbolic Model and Notation}\label{sec:symbolic}

We model the protocol using \tamarin~\cite{meier2013}. It is a symbolic tool for
security-protocol analysis, which tracks a \emph{system} represented as a multiset of facts,
referred to as the state~$S$. The state evolves following user-defined transition \emph{rules}
of the following shape:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rule}
  [\,L\,]\;\rule[0.55ex]{1.6em}{0.4pt}\,[\,A\,]\,\longrightarrow\;[\,R\,]
\end{equation}
with the premises~$L$, the action facts~$A$ and the conclusions~$R$.

A rule of shape~\eqref{eq:rule} can be applied when $L$ unifies with facts in~$S$. It captures
that $L$ is rewritten as $R$ in~$S$, and the action facts~$A$ are appended to the trace.
\tamarin{} features linear and persistent facts; the former are consumed upon rule application,
while the latter (prefixed with \texttt{!}) can be reused arbitrarily many times. The tool also
provides the built-in facts \code{In}, \code{Out} and \code{Fr}, capturing respectively
receiving from and sending to a public (i.e., attacker-controlled) channel, and generating fresh
and unique values (e.g., for nonces). In rules, \texttt{\~{}x} denotes a fresh value and
\texttt{\$A} a public name, such as the identity of a party.

We further use the hashing, signing and natural-numbers built-ins. Hashing provides the
function~$h$, which we use for payment hashes $y = h(x)$ and for the commitment $h(s)$ to a
revocation secret~$s$. Signing provides \code{sign}, \code{verify} and \code{pk}, which we use
for the channel handshake, the commitments and the invoices. Natural numbers provide a sort for
amounts (variables prefixed with \texttt{\%}) with addition, which we use for the per-hop
constraint between the incoming amount, the outgoing amount and the fee
(Sec.~\ref{sec:core-amounts}).

An execution produces a trace of action facts. Security goals are then first-order formulas
over these traces, enabling security queries (over all traces, \code{all-traces} lemmas) and
reachability queries (ensuring that a certain trace exists, \code{exists-trace} lemmas). Such
formulas quantify over messages and over the time points (written \texttt{\#i}) at which action
facts occur. A \emph{restriction} is a formula of the same kind that \tamarin{} imposes on the
model: only the traces that satisfy every restriction are considered.

\section{Formal Modelling Methodology}\label{sec:method}

We model the protocol as a single \tamarin{} theory, \code{lightning.spthy}, with 37 rules and a
single adversary. It contains both the payment-channel protocol (Sec.~\ref{sec:channel}) and
multi-hop routing (Sec.~\ref{sec:routing}); one generic rule, \code{Forward_HTLC}, handles every
forwarding hop, so routes of any length are covered. The routing layer does not abstract the
channel layer away, but the coupling is deliberately thin: commitment transactions carry no HTLC
outputs, and, besides the long-term keys, the two layers interact only through three explicit
interfaces.

\paragraph{Topology and Slot} \code{Lock_Funds_And_Open} mints the persistent facts
\code{!ChannelConnect}, one per direction, which the routing rules \code{Sender_Offer_HTLC},
\code{Forward_HTLC} and \code{Open_Timeout} require. HTLCs are therefore added only on opened
channels (\code{HTLC_On_Opened_Channel}). Opening also mints one linear fact
\code{Mutex(ptr)}: adding an HTLC consumes it, and redeeming or refunding that HTLC returns it.
A channel thus holds at most one in-flight HTLC at a time and can be reused afterwards.

\paragraph{Resolution} Every rule that redeems or refunds an HTLC records the action fact
\code{Old}, and the following restriction lets each HTLC be resolved at most once:
\begin{spthy}
restriction OneResolutionPerHTLC:
    "All ptr y #i #j. Old(ptr, y) @ #i & Old(ptr, y) @ #j
     ==> #i = #j"
\end{spthy}
This is the interface assumption between the two layers. In the protocol, it holds because an
HTLC output of the current commitment transaction can be spent only once on chain. Spending it
again from an older state means publishing a revoked commitment, which lets the counterparty
take the whole channel balance (\code{Publish_Revoked_A/B}, \code{Punish_A/B}). This argument
relies on the counterparty reacting in time and is not mechanised: we claim no composition
theorem. All other abstractions are also explicit, named restrictions
(Sec.~\ref{sec:restrictions}).

\subsection{Threat Model}\label{sec:threat}

\tamarin{} assumes the standard Dolev-Yao adversary model~\cite{dolevyao1983}: the adversary
controls the whole network; it can read, modify and inject messages of arbitrary complexity; it
is, however, constrained to the term algebra under consideration. Our adversary keeps this full
control. Public keys, channel proposals and acknowledgements, and invoices are sent over the
network. The adversary also chooses the values that honest rules read from it: the balances of a
channel update (\code{Update_State}) and, at each hop, the incoming amount, the fee and the
outgoing amount (\code{Forward_HTLC}), subject only to the incoming amount being the outgoing
amount plus the fee. HTLC offers and fulfilments between adjacent hops are linear facts
(\code{Route}, \code{Fulfilled}), that is, authenticated, non-replayable messages between
channel partners.

\paragraph{Key Compromise} Rule \code{Compromise_Ltk} reveals the long-term key of any party at
any time (action fact \code{LtkCompromised}); the adversary can then sign as that party. For
instance, once the receiver's key is compromised, a sender can settle a payment for which the
receiver created no invoice (\code{Forged_Invoice_Requires_Key_Compromise}). The preimage~$x$ is
never sent on the network, so the adversary never learns it (\code{Preimage_Never_Known}). What
compromised parties can do with it is given explicitly by rule \code{AttackerSkip}: two
compromised parties that offered or forwarded HTLCs with the same payment hash may move the
fulfilment of one party's outgoing HTLC to the other party's outgoing HTLC, relaying~$x$ around
the parties between them. This is the wormhole attack~\cite{malavolta2019}
(Sec.~\ref{sec:wormhole}). Otherwise, compromised parties follow the protocol rules, with the
exemptions of Sec.~\ref{sec:restrictions}.

\paragraph{Time} Time is abstract. The timeout of channel \code{ptr} is the event
\code{TimedOut(ptr)} of rule \code{Open_Timeout}. It can happen at any point after the channel
is opened, and at most once per channel (\code{OneTimeoutPerPtr}). The adversary therefore
chooses when timeouts happen relative to honest actions.

\paragraph{Liveness} As mentioned before, the security of the Lightning Network relies on the
liveness of the participants, whether to deter cheating behaviour (Sec.~\ref{par:punishment}) or to
claim HTLCs (Remark~\ref{rmk:claiming}). However, a standard Dolev-Yao adversary has full
control over the network. It can notably keep a party from seeing the network (e.g.,
preventing it from noticing a fraud) or from sending a message (e.g., the defending
transaction), leading to trivial attacks on the Lightning Network~\cite{mccorry2019}. This is
why our model must encode liveness: no honest forwarder can be forcibly put offline. We do so
with \tamarin{} restrictions.

\subsection{Liveness Restrictions}\label{sec:restrictions}

To make the model relevant, we restrict the adversary's control over the order of events to
allow for liveness, and we rule out pathological honest behaviour. Concretely, we impose three
restrictions on the routing layer. We show them without the preprocessor flags
(\code{NO_RBT}, \code{NO_OTF}, \code{NO_IMC}) that remove them for the necessity runs of
Sec.~\ref{sec:timing}.
\begin{spthy}[linerange={1-3,9-14,20-26}]
restriction RedeemBeforeTimeout:
    "All ptr y #i #j.
        Redeemed(ptr, y) @ #i & TimedOut(ptr) @ #j ==> #i < #j"
#endif

#ifdef NO_OTF
// restriction removed for the necessity check
#else
restriction OutgoingTimesOutFirst:
    "All P Prev Next ptrIn ptrOut y #f #k.
        HTLCForwarded(P, Prev, Next, ptrIn, ptrOut, y) @ #f
      & TimedOut(ptrIn) @ #k
      & not (Ex #c. LtkCompromised(P) @ #c & #c < #k)
     ==> Ex #m. TimedOut(ptrOut) @ #m & #m < #k"
#endif

#ifdef NO_IMC
// restriction removed for the necessity check
#else
restriction IntermediaryMustClaim:
    "All P Prev Next ptrIn ptrOut y #f #i #k.
        HTLCForwarded(P, Prev, Next, ptrIn, ptrOut, y) @ #f
      & Redeemed(ptrOut, y) @ #i
      & TimedOut(ptrIn)  @ #k
      & not (Ex #c. LtkCompromised(P) @ #c & #c < #k)
     ==> Ex #l. Redeemed(ptrIn, y) @ #l"
\end{spthy}

\noindent\textbf{Restriction T1} (\code{RedeemBeforeTimeout}). \emph{If an HTLC is redeemed and
its channel times out, the redemption comes first.} This is not a liveness assumption but a
well-formedness condition on timeouts, and it applies to every HTLC.

\noindent\textbf{Restriction T2} (\code{OutgoingTimesOutFirst}). \emph{If the incoming HTLC of a
forwarding hop times out and the forwarder was not compromised before, then its outgoing HTLC
timed out earlier.} This is the decreasing-deadline (CLTV-delta) invariant of
Remark~\ref{rmk:deadlines}: deadlines are not numbers in the main model, and T2 captures their
order.

\noindent\textbf{Restriction T3} (\code{IntermediaryMustClaim}). \emph{If a forwarder's outgoing
HTLC is redeemed and its incoming HTLC times out, and the forwarder was not compromised before
that timeout, then its incoming HTLC is redeemed too.} This is the claiming liveness of
Remarks~\ref{rmk:claiming} and~\ref{rmk:deadlines}. It only constrains traces in which the
incoming HTLC times out, the only ones in which the upstream party can be refunded
(\code{Refund_Requires_Timeout}); by T1, the claim then precedes the timeout.

T2 and T3 do not constrain forwarders compromised before their incoming timeout. The order of a
hop's two timeouts is not assumed separately: \tamarin{} proves from T2 and
\code{OneTimeoutPerPtr} that, if both time out and the forwarder was not compromised before its
incoming timeout, the outgoing one times out first.
\begin{spthy}
lemma ForwardTimeGap [reuse]:
    "All P Prev Next ptrIn ptrOut y #f #j #k.
        HTLCForwarded(P, Prev, Next, ptrIn, ptrOut, y) @ #f
      & TimedOut(ptrOut) @ #j
      & TimedOut(ptrIn)  @ #k
      & not (Ex #c. LtkCompromised(P) @ #c & #c < #k)
     ==> #j < #k"
\end{spthy}
Being restrictions, T1 to T3 remove every trace that violates them, including traces in which an
uncompromised forwarder could not have acted in time; our results say nothing about such traces
(Sec.~\ref{par:liveness-scope}). The liveness of punishment (Sec.~\ref{par:punishment}) is not
encoded: no restriction mentions cheating or punishment.

The six remaining restrictions are structural. \code{Equality} and \code{Inequality} turn the
action facts \code{Eq} and \code{Neq} into constraints, which encodes checks such as signature
verification; \code{OneChannelPerPtr} and \code{OneTimeoutPerPtr} let a channel open and time
out at most once; \code{OneHTLCPerHashPerChannel} lets a payment hash be added at most once per
channel; and \code{OneResolutionPerHTLC} is the interface assumption above.

Sec.~\ref{sec:timing} removes one liveness restriction at a time. Without T3, \tamarin{} finds a
trace with no key compromise in which an intermediary's outgoing HTLC is redeemed while its
incoming one is refunded; T1 is not needed by any of the five liveness lemmas, and T2 is not
needed for intermediary safety. Under all restrictions, we prove the properties of
Secs.~\ref{sec:properties} to~\ref{sec:wormhole} and the bypass theorems for positions 1 to 6
(Sec.~\ref{sec:bypass-fixed}); the bypass theorem for routes of any length
(Sec.~\ref{sec:bypass-any}) is proved without T1, T2 and T3.

\subsection{Structural Limitations}\label{sec:structure}

The Lightning Network is a complex protocol. Verifying it as a single monolithic block quickly
hits scalability problems with verification tools~\cite{grundmann2026}, and \tamarin{} is no
exception. This is not an assumption but an observation: when signatures, an equational theory,
natural-number induction and an unbounded clock are combined, \tamarin{} exhausts memory;
without the unbounded clock, the others are tractable together. We therefore keep the block
clock out of the main theory, which represents timeouts as untimed events ordered by the
restrictions of Sec.~\ref{sec:restrictions}. The block clock and the CLTV arithmetic are
analysed in small auxiliary models that use hashing and natural numbers only.

Our artifact thus consists of one main theory and five small auxiliary models, all in
\code{lightning_aux.spthy} and each selected by a preprocessor flag: the PTLC countermeasure
(\code{-D=PTLC}) and its HTLC baseline (\code{-D=HTLC}), a block-clock model of CLTV deadlines
(\code{-D=CLOCK}), CLTV-delta arithmetic (\code{-D=CLTV}) and the early-timeout race
(\code{-D=TIMEOUT}). Each auxiliary model is self-contained and is not composed with the main
theory; Table~\ref{tab:results} states what each run establishes.

Inside the main theory, HTLC state is deliberately not folded into the commitment transactions:
\code{StateA} and \code{StateB} carry balances and revocation data but no HTLC outputs. This
keeps the channel state independent of the payments routed over it, while one theory and one
adversary cover channels with any number of updates and routes of any length. The price is the
interface assumption \code{OneResolutionPerHTLC}, imposed rather than derived from the channel
mechanics. A mechanised discharge of it, by composition or by refinement as
in~\cite{grundmann2026}, is the natural next step.

Finally, five aspects are deliberate non-goals. \emph{Onion privacy:} each forwarder may pick
any channel partner as its next hop and nothing is encrypted in layers, so the results cover
every route but claim no privacy. \emph{On-chain fees:} publishing a transaction is free, which
separates the protocol logic from fee economics. \emph{Fee policy:} the adversary chooses every
routing fee, so the results hold for any policy. \emph{Wallet capacity:} balances are opaque
terms and HTLC amounts are not checked against them, so the results hold for all amounts.
\emph{Per-HTLC CSV stage:} an HTLC is redeemed or refunded in one step, without a second-stage
transaction, so each HTLC has a single resolution event.
