\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

Grundmann and Hartenstein~\cite{grundmann2026} model-check a stepwise refinement of the
Lightning Network in TLA+; model checking explores a bounded state space. Our \tamarin{} proofs
are symbolic and unbounded: subject to the restrictions of our model
(Sec.~\ref{sec:restrictions}), including three liveness restrictions, they cover any number of
parties, channels, channel updates and payments over routes of any length. \cite{litos2026} presents a formally verified Lightning Network.
% TODO: one sentence on the scope and the tool of [9] and how it differs from our work
% (keep it consistent with the corresponding sentence of the introduction).

Kiayias and Litos~\cite{kiayias2020} give a composable security treatment of the Lightning
Network: they define an ideal functionality for a payment network and prove, in the universal
composability framework, that the Lightning protocol realises it. Their guarantee is
computational and composable, and their proof is by hand. Ours are symbolic but machine-checked
by \tamarin; they include bypass resistance for routes of any length (for single-offer
payments), and they contrast the wormhole under HTLCs with its absence under PTLC-style locks on
a fixed route.

Malavolta et al.~\cite{malavolta2019} describe the wormhole attack on HTLC-based multi-hop
payments and propose anonymous multi-hop locks as a countermeasure; their instantiation with
adaptor signatures is the basis of PTLCs. Adaptor signatures also underlie the generalised
channels of Aumayr et al.~\cite{aumayr2021}, built from limited blockchain scripts. Our main
model reproduces the wormhole (Sec.~\ref{sec:wormhole}), and our auxiliary models check the
countermeasure symbolically on a fixed route (Sec.~\ref{sec:ptlc}).
Smith et al.~\cite{smith2022} formalise agent-skipping attacks on message-forwarding protocols;
the wormhole is an instance of such an attack. Our bypass theorems (Sec.~\ref{sec:bypass}) state
the corresponding security property for Lightning routing: a forwarder at positions 1 to 6 of a
route, or at any position for single-offer payments, can be skipped only if some long-term key
is compromised.

Backes et al.~\cite{backes2017} reason about liveness in cryptographic protocols with a progress
semantics, in which honest processes that can continue without input from the adversary
eventually do, and apply it to fair exchange. We impose liveness as trace restrictions instead
(Sec.~\ref{par:liveness-scope}).

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}

\begin{itemize}[\setlength{\IEEElabelindent}{0pt}]
\item \emph{Abstract timing, liveness as restrictions} (Sec.~\ref{par:liveness-scope}). The main
  model has no clock: \code{Open_Timeout} lets a channel time out at any point allowed by the
  restrictions, and T1, T2 and T3 (the last two only for forwarders not compromised before their
  incoming HTLC times out) stand in for CLTV deadlines and timely claiming. They prune every
  trace that violates them, including traces in which an uncompromised forwarder could not have
  claimed in time; results proved under them say nothing about such runs. Only the 24 lemmas of
  \texttt{make general} are proved with all three removed;
  the necessity runs remove one at a time, and T3 is necessary for intermediary safety
  (Sec.~\ref{sec:timing}). Block heights appear only in the auxiliary CLOCK and CLTV models, which
  are not composed with \code{lightning.spthy}. The progress semantics of~\cite{backes2017}
  would keep the pruned traces, but \tamarin{} 1.10 offers it only for \sapic{} processes.
\item \emph{Scope of the bypass and PTLC results} (Secs.~\ref{sec:bypass-caveats}
  and~\ref{sec:ptlc}). Theorem~\ref{thm:anyhop} needs the single-offer premise; for a hash
  offered more than once, only positions 1 to 6 are covered, under T1, T2 and T3. No bypass
  theorem says whose key is compromised, or when. The countermeasure is checked only on a fixed
  route with three intermediaries, with honest lock set-up, an honest sender and a hash chain in
  place of the adaptor-signature algebra.
\item \emph{No end-to-end amounts.} Each \code{Forward_HTLC} requires its incoming amount to be the outgoing amount plus the fee, but the offer,
  the invoice and the \code{Route} fact carry no amount. Amounts are therefore not chained along
  the path, and we make no end-to-end claims about value, e.g., that the receiver obtains the
  invoiced amount or that the sender pays exactly the path fees.
\item \emph{HTLCs are not part of the commitment transactions.} The channel and routing layers share the theory,
  the adversary and the channel topology (HTLCs are added only on opened channels,
  \code{HTLC_On_Opened_Channel}), but settling or refunding an HTLC does not update the channel
  state. That each HTLC is resolved at most once is assumed as the restriction \code{OneResolutionPerHTLC}, not
  derived from the channel layer; we do not claim a composition theorem. A channel carries at
  most one pending HTLC at a time (\code{Mutex(ptr)}).
\item \emph{Routing abstraction, no privacy.} Offers travel as the private linear fact
  \code{Route}, so the network adversary cannot inject, replay or modify offers between peers; it
  chooses amounts and fees, controls the delivery of invoices and can compromise keys. It never
  learns a preimage (\code{Preimage_Never_Known}): in the main model the wormhole comes from the
  explicit rule \code{AttackerSkip}, which needs both ends of the skip compromised, rather than
  from adversary knowledge. There is no
  onion routing: every forwarder knows its
  predecessor, its successor and the payment hash, and the next hop is any party with which it
  shares a channel. We make no privacy claims.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

We presented one \tamarin{} theory of the Lightning Network that covers the channel layer and
multi-hop HTLC routing over routes of any length, against a Dolev-Yao adversary that can also
compromise long-term keys. We proved that only cheating or compromised parties are punished,
that settlements are authenticated by the receiver's invoice and that preimages stay secret;
under three liveness restrictions, an uncompromised intermediary never has its outgoing HTLC
redeemed while its incoming one is refunded, and payments are atomic in runs without key
compromise. The assumption that intermediaries claim in time is necessary: without it, \tamarin{}
finds a run without any compromise in which an honest intermediary loses. The wormhole is a
\tamarin-checked trace in which two compromised parties bypass an honest one, which is refunded
and earns no fee. Conversely, a bypass requires some key compromise: under the liveness
restrictions at positions 1 to 6 of any route, and, for payments whose hash is offered on a
single channel, at every position even without them. On a fixed route, per-hop locks in the
style of PTLCs rule out the bypass of an honest hop when the sender is honest, whereas a shared
hash lock admits it. Next, we want to replace the liveness restrictions by the progress
semantics of~\cite{backes2017}, so that runs in which an honest party cannot act are analysed
rather than pruned, and to derive \code{OneResolutionPerHTLC} from the channel layer.
