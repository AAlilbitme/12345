\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Bitcoin~\cite{nakamoto2008} is the most important cryptocurrency in terms of
market capitalisation. It is, however, lacking in many usability and
scalability aspects: it is costly, has low bandwidth (only a few dozen
transactions per second), and has high latency. Many protocols strive to
alleviate those shortcomings.

\paragraph{Payment Channel Networks}
Notably, payment channels enable a pair of users to quickly and cheaply perform
arbitrarily many payments by relying on the blockchain only to resolve
disputes. Both agents pool together funds and then iteratively update their
balance. The scripts involved and the protocol itself ensure that no honest
agent loses money, by relying on a revocation mechanism: any party closing a
channel using an outdated favourable state can be punished, consequently losing
all funds invested in the channel. However, payment channels are constrained to
pairs of users. Two unconnected users still need to establish a channel in
order to perform a payment; this is often not cost effective. Thus arose the
concept of payment channel networks: one transitively propagates funds across
payment channels to perform a payment between two users that are part of the
same network but not connected directly. The most popular of such protocols is
perhaps the Lightning Network~\cite{poon2016}, with about \$170M locked on the
network~\cite{oneml2026}.

Due to the sheer complexity of its specification~\cite{bolts}, it suffers from
many attacks~\cite{tikhomirov2020}, \cite{malavolta2019}, \cite{aumayr2021}.
And despite promising ongoing efforts~\cite{grundmann2026}, \cite{litos2026},
it still lacks a machine-checked model that covers both the channel layer and
multi-hop routing, for unboundedly many channels, updates and payments and for
routes of any length. This paper attempts to alleviate that issue.

\paragraph{Protocol Verification}
Designing and analysing cryptographic protocols is a notoriously difficult and
error-prone task, as already illustrated by the various attacks and tentative
fixes on the Lightning Network. Formal methods provide hope for a more robust
verification of protocols, and for automation.

Protocol verification is further split between two main classes of models: the
computational model, inherited from cryptography, where the attackers and the
participants are polynomial-time algorithms and results are typically
probabilistic; and the symbolic (or \emph{Dolev-Yao}) model~\cite{dolevyao1983},
where the attacker has infinite computational power but is limited to a fixed
term algebra. The symbolic model notably abstracts away from cryptography and
focuses on the logic of a protocol, where much of the attacker activity is
typically found.

Although some tools exist to help the cryptographer in the computational model,
such as \textsc{CryptoVerif}~\cite{blanchet2006},
\textsc{EasyCrypt}~\cite{barthe2011}, \textsc{Squirrel}~\cite{baelde2021} or
\textsc{CryptoVampire}~\cite{jeanteur2024}, they are all brittle, hard to use
or narrow in scope. In contrast, symbolic verification enjoys very successful
automated verifiers in the form of \proverif~\cite{blanchet2001} and
\tamarin~\cite{meier2013}. Mechanised proofs now exist for widely used
protocols such as \textsc{WireGuard}~\cite{lipp2019} and
TLS~1.3~\cite{cremers2017}, among many others.
% [17] is a computational (CryptoVerif) proof of WireGuard, not a Tamarin model.
In particular, \tamarin{} is a multiset rewriting-based automated protocol
verifier with a large pedigree of verified protocols, TLS~1.3 among them. It
proves trace properties for an unbounded number of sessions and lets the
modeller state assumptions explicitly, as restrictions on traces; both features
suit a protocol with unboundedly many channels, updates and payments.

\paragraph{Contributions}
All results are checked with \tamarin~1.10. The all-traces statements hold for
every trace of the model that satisfies its restrictions
(Sec.~\ref{sec:method}), that is, for any number of parties, channels,
channel updates and payments.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{One theory, one adversary.} A single \tamarin{} theory
  (\code{lightning.spthy}) covers the channel layer (opening, unboundedly many
  updates, revocation, cooperative and unilateral closing, punishment) and
  multi-hop HTLC routing, where one generic rule, \code{Forward_HTLC}, handles
  every hop of routes of any length. The layers share a Dolev-Yao adversary
  that can also compromise any long-term key at any time
  (Sec.~\ref{sec:threat}), the channel topology and a per-channel slot. Their
  contract, that each HTLC is resolved at most once, is the named restriction
  \code{OneResolutionPerHTLC}, which we justify informally; we claim no
  composition theorem (Sec.~\ref{sec:method}).
\item \emph{Safety.} We prove 32 all-traces safety lemmas, e.g.: a party is
  punished only if it published a revoked state or its key was compromised; a
  sender settles only against an invoice of the receiver, unless the receiver's
  key was compromised; preimages stay secret (our model never sends them); no
  HTLC is both redeemed and refunded. Under three named liveness restrictions,
  T1 to T3, an intermediary whose outgoing HTLC is redeemed has its incoming
  HTLC refunded only if its key was compromised before that HTLC timed out, and
  payment atomicity holds in runs without key compromise. These restrictions
  remove every trace that violates them, and the results say nothing about such
  traces. Ten exists-trace lemmas show that the protocol runs and that attacks
  with compromised keys, such as the wormhole below, are reachable
  (Sec.~\ref{sec:verification}).
\item \emph{The wormhole, as a trace and as a theorem.} \tamarin{} checks (by
  replaying a saved proof) a five-party trace in which the payment settles
  while an honest intermediary is refunded on its outgoing HTLC and earns no
  fee (Sec.~\ref{sec:wormhole}). Conversely, if the sender settles, a forwarder
  on its route is refunded on its outgoing HTLC only if some long-term key is
  compromised: we prove this for each of the first six hops of a route of any
  length, and for every hop if the payment hash is offered only once. The
  latter is proved without the liveness restrictions, so it also holds with
  them. Neither result says whose key is compromised, or when
  (Sec.~\ref{sec:bypass}).
\item \emph{Necessity of the liveness assumptions.} Without T3
  (\code{IntermediaryMustClaim}), \tamarin{} finds a trace without key
  compromise in which an intermediary's outgoing HTLC is redeemed while its
  incoming HTLC is refunded. Without T1, all five liveness lemmas we check
  remain provable; without T2, both intermediary-safety lemmas do. The
  CLTV-delta ordering of timeouts (\code{ForwardTimeGap}) is a lemma derived
  from T2 (Sec.~\ref{sec:timing}).
\item \emph{Countermeasure.} On a fixed route of three intermediaries, per-hop
  locks derived with secret tweaks, a symbolic stand-in for point time-locked
  contracts (PTLCs)~\cite{malavolta2019}, prevent bypassing the uncompromised
  middle hop unless the sender is compromised; with a common payment hash, the
  bypass is reachable (Sec.~\ref{sec:ptlc}).
\item \emph{Reproducibility.} One command (\texttt{make all}) reproduces all
  results: the 55 lemmas of the main model, the 24 of the any-length theorem,
  the 21 of the auxiliary models, and the necessity runs
  (Sec.~\ref{sec:verified}).
\end{itemize}
Unlike the TLA+ model checking of~\cite{grundmann2026}, which explores finite
instances, our all-traces results hold for unboundedly many channels, updates
and payments, and for routes of any length.
