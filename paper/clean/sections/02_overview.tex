\section{Protocol Overview and Threat Model}\label{sec:overview}

% Boxed output numbers of Fig. 2 (used in this section only).
\DeclareRobustCommand{\txout}[1]{\mbox{\setlength{\fboxsep}{0.8pt}\setlength{\fboxrule}{0.4pt}\fbox{\scriptsize\textbf{#1}}}}

The Lightning Network has two main parts. The \emph{payment channels} transfer
funds quickly and efficiently between two participants with reduced interaction
with a blockchain, after a comparatively expensive setup; the \emph{routing}
protocol scales these pairwise payments to a whole network. In the channel,
revocation keys protect against \emph{outdated states}; in routing, a payment
hash and a deadline control the \emph{transfer}. They meet only in the
commitment transaction, where an in-flight payment is an extra output subject
to the same revocation as the rest of the channel.

\subsection{Payment Channels}\label{sec:channel}
A payment channel lets two agents \emph{commit} and \emph{revoke} a shared
``state'' tracking the balance of a multi-sig~\cite{bip11} \emph{common fund},
effectively transferring coins between them. It is represented by valid
transactions spending that fund. Careful signing (restricting transactions to
agreed-upon shapes) and \emph{punishing} ensure that no agent can publish an
old, favourable state: a participant may unconditionally retrieve \emph{all}
funds in the channel if its counterparty puts an old state on chain. In the
Lightning Network, the state also tracks in-flight payments propagating beyond
the channel, carried by \emph{hash time-locked contracts} (HTLCs;
Sec.~\ref{sec:routing}).

\subsubsection{The Transactions}\label{sec:transactions}
Fig.~\ref{fig:transaction} shows the commitment transaction held by Alice in an
Alice-Bob channel, which enables unilateral closing; our model abstracts it,
but its shape is worth keeping in mind. Outputs \txout{3} and \txout{4}
redistribute the common fund between Alice and Bob; Outputs \txout{1} and
\txout{2} lock the funds of in-flight payments. Unlike Bob's
Output~\txout{3}, Output~\txout{4} is redeemable by Alice only after a relative
timelock (CSV, for the \texttt{CheckSequenceVerify} opcode), which gives Bob
the opportunity to redeem it with Alice's \emph{revocation key} ($rk_A$),
initially hidden from Bob. Output~\txout{1} (resp.~\txout{2}) holds in-flight
funds flowing towards Bob (resp.\ Alice). Besides punishment, these outputs are
governed by a \emph{payment hash} and a timeout: to \emph{fulfil} the transfer,
the receiver (Bob for Output~\txout{1}) needs the preimage of the payment hash;
to prevent funds from being locked up, the output reverts to a regular output at
block height $h_i$, an absolute timeout set with CLTV (the
\texttt{CheckLockTimeVerify} opcode). The real Lightning Network separates the
CLTV and CSV conditions with a two-stage construction, which
Fig.~\ref{fig:transaction} collapses into a single output per HTLC. Our model
keeps counterparts of Outputs~\txout{3} and~\txout{4} and of the revocation
path only; HTLCs are resolved in the routing layer (Sec.~\ref{sec:method}).

\subsubsection{The Protocol}\label{sec:protocol}
The lifecycle of a channel has five phases, which correspond, left to right, to
Fig.~\ref{fig:lifecycle}.

\paragraph{Opening}\label{par:opening}
To prevent griefing attacks, the opening establishes a first committed state
before the common fund. After an unmodelled negotiation of the inputs of the
common fund, Alice and Bob each sample a random revocation key ($rk^0_A$ and
$rk^0_B$) and exchange their hashes. Relying on SegWit~\cite{bip141}, both
compute the TXID of the common fund, an on-chain output locked by a 2-of-2
multisig~\cite{bip11}, and exchange the signatures needed to spend it (e.g.,
Bob signs the outputs of Fig.~\ref{fig:transaction};
Sec.~\ref{sec:core-channels} describes our abstraction). The witnesses for the
common fund are then exchanged and the funding transaction is published; once
it is on chain, the channel is ready.

\paragraph{Update}\label{par:update}
The parties pay each other off chain by exchanging signed commitment
transactions. They first commit to a new state $n$ as in the opening (sampling
$rk^n_A$ and $rk^n_B$, exchanging their hashes and the signatures), and only
then revoke state $n-1$ by disclosing its revocation keys (e.g., $A$ sends
$rk^{n-1}_A$ to $B$). Reversed, these steps would leave a party nothing to
publish if its counterparty stopped responding (old state revoked, new one
unsigned).

\paragraph{Revocation}\label{par:revocation}
Alice retires state $n$ by giving $rk^n_A$ to Bob, proving that she will not use
it: the key is useless unless Alice publishes that state. A commitment not yet
revoked is a valid unilateral close, publishable without penalty; only
publishing a revoked one triggers punishment.

\paragraph{Closing}\label{par:closing}
A \emph{cooperative} close is one transaction, signed by both parties, that
sends the agreed final balance directly to their wallets: no scripts and no
delay, the cheapest outcome for both. In a \emph{unilateral} close, either
party publishes its current commitment alone; the CSV delay on its own
Output~\txout{4} is a punishment window in which the counterparty can check
whether the state was revoked and, if so, take that output outright.
Unilateral closing thus disadvantages the publisher, and an honest party uses
it only when its counterparty has stopped responding.

\paragraph{Punishment}\label{par:punishment}
If a party publishes an old state, whose revocation key its counterparty
knows, the latter can take the whole channel balance via the punishment
paths of Fig.~\ref{fig:transaction}. Punishment is an \emph{active}
defence: the defender must notice the fraud, send the punishment transaction
and have it accepted within the CSV window. Failure to react makes the protocol
trivially insecure~\cite{mccorry2019}.

\subsection{Multi-hop HTLC Routing}\label{sec:routing}
When the sender and recipient do \emph{not} share a channel, the payment is
routed through intermediaries as in Fig.~\ref{fig:inflight}: it is a
\emph{multi-hop} payment. As intermediaries are untrusted, each hop relays the
payment in an HTLC conditioned on a single shared secret, the \emph{payment
hash}, so that the payment either completes along the entire path or fails;
the wormhole attack (below) breaks this for the hops between colluding
intermediaries. Intermediaries earn a routing fee.

\begin{example}[Path]\label{ex:path}
We will use the running example where $S$ sends money to $R$ with the
following path:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:path}
  S \to F_1 \to \cdots \to F_n \to R
\end{equation}
\end{example}

\paragraph{Locking}
The receiver ($R$ in Ex.~\ref{ex:path}) generates a preimage $x$, keeps it
strictly secret, and sends the sender ($S$) an invoice with the hash $y = H(x)$
and the requested amount $v$. The sender adds an HTLC to its channel with
$F_1$, locking funds $v_0$ that a preimage $x'$ with $H(x') = y$ unlocks before
the deadline $d_0$ (as in Fig.~\ref{fig:transaction}); after $d_0$, the payer
may reclaim them. Each forwarder in turn locks funds under the same hash, with
decreasing funds and deadlines, the routing fee $v_{k-1} - v_k$ going to $F_k$:
\begin{align}
  v_0 > v_1 > \cdots > v_n &= v \label{eq:amounts}\\
  d_0 > d_1 > \cdots > d_n & \label{eq:deadlines}
\end{align}
The funds have not moved yet: each HTLC is an additional conditional output in
the two parties' current commitment transaction. $F_i$ can safely lock funds,
since its incoming HTLC has a larger amount and a later deadline: if the
outgoing HTLC is claimed, $F_i$ learns $x$ and still has time to unlock the
incoming funds, keeping the difference as fee (Sec.~\ref{sec:core-amounts}
gives our abstraction of amounts and deadlines).

\paragraph{Release Round}
The receiver reveals $x$ to the last forwarder to claim the final HTLC. Since
every HTLC has the same preimage, each intermediary learns $x$ when the next hop
claims from it and uses it to claim its incoming HTLC, keeping its fee: $x$
propagates backward hop by hop until the sender's HTLC is settled. If a party
fails to forward it and the timelock expires, the funds return to the party
that locked them. Once $x$ is released, all HTLCs are settled if every
intermediary claims its incoming HTLC in time; one that fails to do so after
its outgoing HTLC was redeemed loses the forwarded amount, while the parties
downstream are paid. Hence forwarders need liveness
(Remark~\ref{rmk:deadlines}).

\begin{remark}[Claiming and Liveness]\label{rmk:claiming}
Claiming can happen by mutual negotiation or by unilateral closing. Notably,
unilateral closing also publishes $x$, as it is leaked by the closing
transactions redeeming the HTLC.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Decreasing Deadlines and Liveness]\label{rmk:deadlines}
Consider $F_i$ holding an incoming HTLC with deadline $d_{i-1}$ and an outgoing
one with deadline $d_i$. If $F_{i+1}$ unilaterally claims the outgoing HTLC at
$d_i$, $F_i$ learns $x$ as late as $d_i$ and is left the window
$(d_i, d_{i-1})$ to settle its incoming one. This gap, the CLTV delta, is
precisely the time reserved for $F_i$ to recover the funds: in case of
unilateral close, forwarders need liveness to claim their incoming HTLC in this
window~\cite{mccorry2019}.
\end{remark}

Our model imposes this liveness as restriction T3
(Sec.~\ref{sec:restrictions}), without which intermediary safety fails
(Sec.~\ref{sec:timing}). Finally, as all HTLCs of a payment share the hash $y$,
two colluding intermediaries can pass $x$ around the hops between them. In
this wormhole attack~\cite{malavolta2019}, the payment completes while the
bypassed hops are refunded and earn no fee (Secs.~\ref{sec:wormhole}
and~\ref{sec:bypass}).
